Most people can sense when a draft came from an assistant, even if they can't say why. The feeling usually comes from a handful of habits that show up together.

## Even sentence length

AI drafts tend to hold a steady rhythm, with sentence after sentence of similar length. Each one is fine. Together they drone. People vary length without thinking about it: a long sentence that builds up to a point, then a short one.

## Safe word choice

Language models pick likely words, and likely words are often generic. "Significant", "various" and "important" fit almost anywhere, which is why they carry so little.

## Signposting everywhere

Assistants explain the structure of their own argument: "firstly", "moreover", "in conclusion". Good writers lean more on the order of their ideas and less on labels.

## Hedging and balance

"While there are many benefits, it is important to consider the challenges." Assistants tend to stay even-handed, so they rarely commit. Readers came for a view, or at least a clear recommendation.

## No lived detail

This one matters most. A model hasn't run your campaign or met your client. It writes the shape of an example without the example: "many companies have seen great results". That vagueness is what really makes text feel empty.

## What fixes what

| Habit | Can rules fix it? | What works |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Stock words and openers | Yes, mostly | An editor like [CleanDraft](/) |
| Stiff tone | Partly | Contractions, plainer words |
| Even rhythm | A little | Reading aloud, cutting by hand |
| Hedging | No | Deciding what you think |
| No lived detail | No | Adding your own facts and stories |

We built a rules engine, and we'll be plain about this: the top two rows are its job, and the bottom three are yours. An edit can clear away the noise so that your own material stands out, but it can't supply that material.

## A two-minute test

Read your draft aloud. Mark every spot where you run out of breath, get bored, or say something you would never say to a colleague. Those marks are your edit list. Then, for each paragraph, ask: "Could this appear in a competitor's article unchanged?" If it could, add something only you know or cut the paragraph.
