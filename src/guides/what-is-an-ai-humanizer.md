An AI humanizer is a tool that edits AI-written text so it reads more like something a person wrote. You paste in a draft from ChatGPT or another assistant, and you get back a version with less stiff, repetitive wording. We make one, so read this as an explanation from someone with a stake in it. We've tried to be straight about the limits.

## The two types

Humanizers work in one of two ways.

| | AI rewriters | Rule-based editors |
| --- | --- | --- |
| How they work | A second AI model rewrites your text | Written rules find and replace known patterns |
| How much changes | Most of the text | Only what a rule matches |
| Can you see what changed? | Usually not | Yes, edit by edit |
| Risk to your facts | Can alter or add details | Low, since only matched phrases change |
| Where your text goes | Usually to the company's servers | Can run on your own device |
| Depth of rewrite | Deep | Light |

Most humanizers are the first type. [CleanDraft](/) is the second. Neither is better at everything: a rewriter changes far more, and a rules editor is more predictable and can show its work.

## What a humanizer can do

- Remove stock phrases and filler transitions
- Replace stiff words with everyday ones
- Add contractions and loosen the tone
- Vary sentence openings and, to a degree, sentence length

## What no humanizer can do

- **Check facts.** If the draft is wrong, the humanized version is wrong too.
- **Add your experience.** Real examples, numbers and opinions have to come from you.
- **Fix a weak argument.** Better wording on a thin idea is still a thin idea.
- **Change who wrote it.** Editing doesn't turn AI-generated work into your own work for the purposes of a school or employer's rules.

## Do they get past AI detectors?

Many humanizers advertise this. Be skeptical of any tool that promises it.

AI detectors estimate how likely it is that text was machine-written, and they make mistakes in both directions, including flagging text that people wrote. Detectors are updated regularly, so a tool that changed a score last month may not this month. A promise of "100% undetectable" isn't one anyone can keep.

We don't make claims about detectors, and we don't tune our rules against them. If your goal is to hide AI use where it isn't allowed, a humanizer doesn't make that honest or safe. If your goal is a draft that reads well, that's what these tools are for.

## How to choose one

Ask five questions:

1. **Where does my text go?** Check whether it's sent to a server and whether it's stored. This matters for work documents.
2. **Can I see what changed?** If not, you'll need to reread the whole text to check it.
3. **Does it protect facts?** Test it with a paragraph full of numbers, names and a quote. Compare the output carefully.
4. **What does it promise?** Be wary of guarantees about detectors.
5. **What does "free" mean?** Many tools limit free use to a few hundred words, then ask you to pay.

## Is using one cheating?

It depends on the rules you're under and on what you do with it. Tidying the wording of your own draft, where AI help is allowed, is ordinary editing. Using a tool to pass off AI-generated work as your own, where that's banned, is dishonest whatever the tool is called. If you're unsure, ask, and see our guide to [using and disclosing AI honestly](/guides/disclose-ai-use).

Humanizers work for what they're actually good at: cleaning up the surface habits of AI writing. They don't replace checking your facts or adding your own thinking. To do the whole job by hand, follow our [step-by-step method](/guides/how-to-humanize-ai-text).
